      How the simulator works
      
    
      Warm condenser water from the chiller (state 1) is sprayed over the fill at the top of the tower and falls against the outdoor air (state 3) that the fan draws in at the bottom. A small part of the water evaporates; that takes heat from the rest, which collects in the basin and goes back to the chiller cooler (state 2). The air leaves at the top warmer and nearly saturated (state 4), and makeup water (state 5) replaces what was lost. The simulator writes the mass and energy balances of the Chapter 12 slides per kilogram of dry air, with the moist-air properties of Psychrometricsnew.m.
      

    
      Equations
      
    
      
      
      Outdoor air, state 3From T3 and φ3 (or the wet bulb): humidity ratio, enthalpy per kg of dry air and wet-bulb temperature T*, as in Psychrometricsnew.m (ASHRAE eqs. 6, 22, 32 and 35).
      
      
      Cold water, state 2The approach is how close the tower brings the water to the outdoor wet bulb, the lowest temperature evaporation can reach. The range is the cooling of the water.
      
      
      Heat rejectedThe chiller’s condenser heat, carried by the water. In US units 1 gal/min of water is 500 lb/h; a cooling-tower ton is 15,000 Btu/h (one ton of cooling plus the compressor work).
      
      
      Mass balancesSlides: the dry air passes through; the evaporated water leaves with the air and is replaced by makeup.
      
      
      Energy balance and exit air, state 4Steady flow, no heat loss, fan work neglected (Example 12.15). The exit air is assumed saturated (φ4 = 100%, adjustable), which fixes T4 and ω4.
      
      
      FanThe fan moves a volume of air measured at the fan, in the exit air. Fan laws at fan speed N: airflow ∝ N, pressure ∝ N2, power ∝ N3.
      
      
      Water useDrift: droplets carried out with the air, a small fraction d of the circulating water. Blowdown keeps the dissolved minerals at C times their level in the makeup water (cycles of concentration).
      
      
      Is there enough air?Heat and water move from the water surface (saturated air at the water temperature, hs) to the air (ha). The air line must stay below the saturation curve; the Merkel number shows how large a tower that takes.
      
    
      
    
      Moist-air properties from the ASHRAE Handbook—Fundamentals (2013, SI), chapter 1, as coded in Psychrometricsnew.m; balances as in Moran & Shapiro, Fundamentals of Engineering Thermodynamics, chapter 12; driving force and tower characteristic after Merkel, as in the ASHRAE Handbook—HVAC Systems and Equipment, cooling towers chapter.
      

    
      The tower in this simulator, and good habits
      
    
      
      
        
        
      • The approach is an input. Real towers reach a larger approach when the air is cooler, the water flow higher or the fan slower. Here you set it, and the simulator checks that the air can carry the heat away: if the air line crosses the saturation curve, the approach is impossible at that airflow.
        • 
        
      • Tower size. Each tower is rated for 3 gal/min of water per ton, cooled from 95 °F to 85 °F with a 78 °F wet bulb (the usual rating point), at the design water-to-air ratio L/G. That fixes its full-speed airflow and fan power.
        • 
        
      • Why evaporation, not heat transfer to the air? On a hot day the air can be warmer than the water, yet the water still cools: evaporating 1 kg of water takes about 2,400 kJ, so roughly 1% of the flow evaporates for every 10 °F (5.6 °C) of range.
        • 
      
      
      
        
        
      • Plume. The warm, saturated exit air mixes with the outdoor air along a straight line on the chart (adiabatic mixing, as in the slides). If that line crosses the saturation curve, water condenses into the visible fog seen above towers on cool mornings.
        • 
        
      • Water treatment. Evaporation leaves the minerals behind. More cycles of concentration mean less blowdown and makeup, but more scale and corrosion risk.
        • 
        
      • Assumptions. Steady flow, no heat loss, liquid water with cp = 4.186 kJ/kg·K (1 Btu/lb·°F), 8.33 lb per gallon, fan work and drift left out of the energy balance, fan efficiency constant over speed.

      Try it yourself
      
    
        
      
      1. At the default rating point, confirm the range of 10 °F and the heat rejected of about 500 tons. Then compute the makeup water of the slides, ṁ5 = ṁa(ω4 − ω3), from the states table.
        2. 
      
      2. Compare the evaporation for the desert, Oklahoma and Gulf Coast examples, all with about the same heat rejected. Why does the dry climate evaporate the most water?
        3. 
      
      3. Slow the fan in steps of 10%. Track the fan power (the cube law) and the exit air temperature. At what speed can the air no longer carry the heat? What does the enthalpy diagram show there?
        4. 
      
      4. Raise the cycles of concentration from 2 to 6. How many gallons a day does that save? Why does the evaporation not change?
        5. 
      
      5. Load Cool, humid morning and find the fog on the psychrometric chart. Which change makes the plume disappear: a warmer morning, drier air, or a lower exit relative humidity?
        6. 
      
      6. Lower the approach from 10 °F to 4 °F at the rating point and watch the Merkel number KaV/L. Why do small approaches need very large towers?
        7. 
      
      7. Move the same tower to Denver. Why does the fan move less dry air there, and what happens to the exit air and the water use?
        8. 
      
      8. Run the MATLAB lines under the step-by-step solution and reproduce T4 and the makeup water with Psychrometricsnew.m.
