      How the simulator works
      
    
      In a straight pipe the wall shear stress costs the flow pressure. A force balance on a length ℓ of pipe gives Δp = 4ℓτw/D, and writing the wall shear in terms of the friction factor f turns it into the Darcy–Weisbach equation. The friction factor depends on two numbers only, the Reynolds number and the relative roughness ε/D; the Moody chart plots that relation, and the Colebrook equation is the formula behind its turbulent curves.
      

    
      Equations
      
    
      
      
      Velocity and Reynolds numberAverage velocity from the flow rate; ν = μ/ρ is the kinematic viscosity.
      
      
      Major (pipe) lossDarcy–Weisbach. The friction factor is f = 4τw/(½ρV2); hL is the head loss and Δp the pressure drop in a horizontal pipe.
      
      
      Laminar flow, Re < 2100From the parabolic (Poiseuille) velocity profile; independent of roughness.
      
      
      Turbulent flow, Re > 4000The Colebrook equation, solved by iteration (as in Colebrook.m). Smooth pipes: ε/D = 0. For a smooth pipe at Re below about 105, Blasius gives f ≈ 0.316/Re1/4; Haaland’s explicit formula is within about 2% of Colebrook.
      
      
      Transition, 2100 < Re < 4000The flow can switch between laminar and turbulent, and the chart has no curve. The simulator joins the laminar value at Re = 2100 to the Colebrook value at Re = 4000 with a straight line on the log-log chart, and flags the result as uncertain.
      
      
      Wholly turbulent (fully rough) flowAt high Re the friction factor stops changing with Re. The dashed line on the chart is Moody’s boundary of that zone.
      
      
      Type II: unknown flow ratef depends on V through Re, so assume f, find V, compute Re, read a new f, and repeat. Re√f is known from the data, so Colebrook can also give f directly.
      
      
      Type III: unknown diameterBoth Re and ε/D depend on D. Assume f, find D, compute Re and ε/D, read a new f, and repeat; D changes only with f1/5, so it settles quickly.

Water properties: density from Kell (1975), viscosity fitted to IAPWS data (within 0.2% from 32 to 212 °F). Air: ideal gas at 1 atm with Sutherland’s law.       Good habits
      
    
      
      
        
        
      • Absolute and relative roughness. ε has units of length; the chart needs ε/D, which has none. The same material is relatively rougher in a small pipe.
        • 
        
      • Reading the chart. Find Re on the bottom axis, go up to the curve of your ε/D (interpolate between curves), then across to f on the left. Below Re = 2100 use the laminar line, whatever the roughness.
        • 
        
      • Pressure drop or head loss? hL is a height of the flowing fluid: 10 psi is 23 ft of water but about 19,000 ft of air. Pumps are rated in head, so hL is what a pump curve needs.
        • 
      
      
      
        
        
      • Trial and error. In Types II and III the unknown sets Re, which sets f. Start with a guess (f = 0.02 is typical, or the fully rough value) and repeat until f stops changing; it usually takes two to four rounds.
        • 
        
      • Assumptions. Steady, incompressible, fully developed flow in a straight, round, horizontal pipe of constant diameter, without fittings (minor losses) or elevation change. For air, keep Δp well below 10% of the absolute pressure.
        • 
        
      • Design margin. A Type III answer is a minimum: pick the next larger standard pipe, and allow for roughness growing as the pipe ages.
