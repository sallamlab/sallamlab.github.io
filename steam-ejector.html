Steam ejector simulator

A steam ejector is a vacuum pump with no moving parts: a supersonic jet of motive steam drags low-pressure vapor along and compresses it. Change the motive steam, the suction pressure and the back pressure, and watch how much vapor the ejector entrains.

      How the simulator works

      The simulator uses the one-dimensional ejector model of Huang, Chang, Wang and Petrenko (1999), applied to steam treated as an ideal gas with γ = 1.30 and R = 461.5 J/kg·K. Both streams enter as saturated vapor. The motive steam expands through a converging-diverging nozzle to supersonic speed. Its jet forms a converging duct for the entrained vapor inside the constant-area mixing tube, and the entrained vapor accelerates until it reaches Mach 1 at a cross-section called the hypothetical throat, y–y. The two streams then mix at constant pressure, pass through a normal shock, and slow down in the diffuser. Four coefficients account for friction and mixing losses.

      1. Motive nozzle. The nozzle is choked, so the flow rate depends only on the upstream state and the throat area At. This is why a motive pressure and a flow rate together fix the throat diameter.
      2. Entrained vapor at the hypothetical throat. With the entrained stream choked (Msy = 1), its pressure at y–y is a fixed fraction of the suction pressure, 0.546 for γ = 1.30:
      3. Primary core at y–y. The jet expands isentropically until its pressure equals that of the entrained stream, Ppy = Psy. The area-Mach relation, reduced by the coefficient φp, gives the area the jet occupies:
      4. Entrained flow. The vapor passes through what is left of the mixing tube, Asy = A3 − Apy. The entrainment ratio is ω = ṁs/ṁp.
      5. Mixing. A momentum balance with the loss coefficient φm and an energy balance give the velocity, temperature and Mach number of the mixed stream:
      6. Shock and diffuser. A normal shock brings the mixed stream to subsonic speed and the diffuser recovers the rest of the kinetic energy. The result is the highest back pressure the ejector can hold while both streams stay choked, the critical back pressure Pc*:

      Three operating modes

      Double chokingPc ≤ Pc*, critical modeBoth streams are choked. The entrained flow is set by steps 1 to 4 and does not change with the back pressure; only the shock moves along the mixing tube. Ejectors are designed to run here.
      Single chokingPc* < Pc < PcoOnly the motive nozzle is choked. The entrained stream is subsonic and its flow falls as the back pressure rises. The simulator solves the same equations with Msy < 1, chosen so that the diffuser exit pressure equals Pc, keeping the area Asy of the critical mode.
      Back-flowPc ≥ Pco, malfunctionThe back pressure is too high for the jet to overcome. The entrained flow stops and steam flows back into the suction line.

      Huang et al. treat only the critical mode. The single-choking branch is an extension of the same equations, and how fast the entrained flow falls depends on how the pressure recovery of the mixed stream is modeled. The shock closure applies the normal-shock relations of step 6 and gives a gradual fall; the momentum closure uses a momentum balance over the full mixing-tube area and gives a steep break-down. Measured break-down pressures usually lie between the two, 10 to 30% above Pc*, so treat Pc* as the design limit.

      Loss coefficients

      ηp = 0.95Isentropic coefficient of the motive nozzle (step 1).
      ηs = 0.85Isentropic coefficient of the entrained flow (step 4).
      φp = 0.88Loss of the primary core between the nozzle exit and y–y (step 3).
      φm = 0.91 to 0.95by area ratio A3/AtMixing loss in the momentum balance (step 5): 0.80/φp for A3/At > 8.3, 0.82/φp between 6.9 and 8.3, and 0.84/φp below 6.9. With these values the model reproduces both the entrainment ratios and the critical back pressures reported by Huang et al.

      Limits of the model

      • Calibrated on a refrigerant. The coefficients were fitted to 11 ejectors running on R141b. For steam they have not been re-tuned, so expect the entrainment ratio to be within about ±20% and the critical back pressure within a few percent.
      • Ideal gas, no condensation. Real steam partly condenses as it expands in the nozzle (wet steam). The ideal-gas model ignores this, which is why the jet temperatures in the table of flow states are far below freezing.
      • Nozzle area ratio. In this model the jet expands isentropically from the nozzle inlet state to the pressure at y–y whatever the nozzle exit area, so the exit-to-throat area ratio changes the nozzle exit state but not the entrained flow. A real nozzle that is far from matched loses efficiency through shocks.
      • One-dimensional. The lengths of the mixing tube and diffuser, the nozzle position and the shape of the suction chamber do not appear. They matter in a real ejector and are studied with CFD and experiments.
      • The shock in the picture is illustrative. The model gives the states before and after the shock at the critical point, not its position. The picture moves it upstream as the back pressure approaches Pc*, as CFD and experiments show.
      • The jet can fill the tube. If the expanded jet is as large as the mixing tube (Apy ≥ A3) there is no room for vapor and the entrained flow is zero at any back pressure.

      Try it yourself

      1. Start from the default and raise the back pressure slowly. At what pressure does the entrained flow start to fall? Compare it with Pc* and explain why the flow did not change before that.
      2. Lower the suction pressure from 10 to 8 kPa. By what percentage does the entrained flow fall? Which term of the equation in step 4 explains most of it?
      3. Keep the flow rate at 50 kg/h and raise the motive pressure from 300 to 1000 kPa. Watch the throat diameter, the entrained flow and Pc*. Is high-pressure steam worth more than low-pressure steam?
      4. Switch to Fixed nozzle and lower the motive pressure until the ejector leaves double choking. Why is a sagging boiler pressure dangerous for a vacuum system?
      5. Make the mixing tube larger, then smaller. One change raises the entrained flow and the other raises Pc*. Explain the trade-off, then press Size the mixing tube for this duty.
      6. Switch between the shock and momentum closures with the back pressure 10% above Pc*. How different are the two answers, and which would you trust for a design?
      7. Check one row of the step-by-step solution by hand: use the isentropic relations to find the Mach number of the jet at y–y from Pg and Ppy.
