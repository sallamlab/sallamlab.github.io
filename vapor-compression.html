      How the simulator works
      
    
      Heat does not flow from a cold space to a warm one by itself. The mechanical vapor compression cycle moves it there with four components: the refrigerant boils in the evaporator at a temperature below the cold space and absorbs heat from it; the compressor raises its pressure, and with it the temperature at which it condenses; it condenses in the condenser at a temperature above the warm surroundings and gives the heat up; and the expansion valve drops the pressure back to the evaporator pressure. On the pressure–enthalpy diagram the two heat exchangers are horizontal lines (constant pressure), the valve is a vertical line (constant enthalpy), and every energy transfer per kilogram of refrigerant is a horizontal distance.
      

    
      Equations
      
    
      
      
      PressuresThe evaporator and condenser temperatures are saturation temperatures; each one fixes a pressure. No pressure drop in the heat exchangers or the piping.
      
      
      State 1, compressor inletSaturated vapor, or vapor superheated above the evaporator temperature so that no liquid reaches the compressor.
      
      
      Compressor (Eq. 10.4 and 6.48)Adiabatic. State 2s is where an isentropic compression would end; the isentropic efficiency gives the actual state 2.
      
      
      Condenser (Eq. 10.5)The refrigerant leaves as saturated liquid, or subcooled below the condenser temperature.
      
      
      Expansion valve (Eq. 10.6)Throttling: no work, no heat transfer, so the enthalpy does not change. Part of the liquid flashes to vapor.
      
      
      Evaporator (Eq. 10.3)The refrigerating effect. The energy balance of the whole cycle follows.
      
      
      Coefficient of performance (Eq. 10.7 and 10.10)β when the cycle cools, γ when it heats. The same cycle has both.
      
      
      Carnot limit (Eq. 10.1 and 10.9)Absolute temperatures. The simulator uses the two refrigerant temperatures, the most favorable choice for the cycle; the real limit between the cold space and the warm surroundings is higher still.
      
      
      Power and capacityWith the refrigerant mass flow rate. One ton of refrigeration is 211 kJ/min (200 Btu/min).
      
    
      
    
      Equation numbers as in Moran, Shapiro, Boettner and Bailey, Fundamentals of Engineering Thermodynamics, chapter 10. Refrigerant properties are computed in your browser by the open-source CoolProp library (Bell, Wronski, Quoilin and Lemort, Ind. Eng. Chem. Res. 53, 2498–2508, 2014; coolprop.org), which is why the page takes a moment to load. R-410A is a blend that CoolProp treats as a single (pseudo-pure) fluid: at one pressure its boiling and condensing temperatures differ by about 0.1 K, so the evaporator temperature is taken at the saturated-vapor side and the condenser temperature at the saturated-liquid side.
      

    
      Good habits
      
    
      
      
        
        
      • Read the diagram the right way. Pressure lines are horizontal and enthalpy is read on the horizontal axis. Inside the dome the temperature lines are horizontal too; in the vapor region they bend down, and the entropy lines run up and to the right. Do not mix the two up.
        • 
        
      • Find 2s first. Follow the entropy line through state 1 up to the condenser pressure. Then use the efficiency: the actual work is the isentropic work divided by ηc, so state 2 lies to the right of 2s.
        • 
        
      • The valve is a vertical line. h4 = h3. It is not isentropic: throttling is irreversible and the entropy goes up.
        • 
      
      
      
        
        
      • COP, not efficiency. β and γ are larger than 1 because the cycle moves heat instead of making it. Use big h minus small h so that every energy transfer is positive.
        • 
        
      • Carnot is a limit, not a method. Use absolute temperatures, and use it only to check that your answer is below it. The COP of an actual cycle comes from the enthalpies.
        • 
        
      • Watch the units. kW is kJ/s; 1 MPa = 10 bar = 1000 kPa; to get tons, multiply kW by 60 and divide by 211.
        • 
      
      
    
      
  
      

      


      
  
      
    
      Try it yourself
      
    
        
      
      1. Load the ideal cycle. Find states 1, 2, 3 and 4 on your own R-410A chart, read the four enthalpies and compute β. How close are you to the simulator?
        2. 
      
      2. Lower the compressor efficiency from 100% to 60%. Which state points move, and which stay? Why does the refrigerating effect qL stay the same while β drops?
        3. 
      
      3. Raise the condenser temperature from 35 to 55 °C (a hotter day). What happens to the compressor work, to qL and to β? Explain both changes from the shape of the diagram.
        4. 
      
      4. Add 10 K of subcooling to the ideal cycle. Which way does state 4 move, and why does β go up at no cost in compressor work?
        5. 
      
      5. Add 10 K of superheat. What happens to the discharge temperature T2 and to β? Why does a real system still need some superheat?
        6. 
      
      6. Switch to the heat pump and lower the evaporator temperature from 5 to −25 °C (a colder day). Plot γ against the evaporator temperature. Why does the cycle “squeeze” on the diagram?
        7. 
      
      7. A power plant turns about half of its fuel energy into electricity. At what γ does a heat pump deliver more heat per unit of fuel than a gas furnace? Find the evaporator temperature where your heat pump reaches it.
        8. 
      
      8. Compare R-410A, R-134a, propane (R-290) and ammonia between the same two temperatures. Which gives the highest β? Which has the highest pressures, and the hottest discharge?
        9. 
      
      9. Load the lecture R-134a example and set the mass flow to 0.08 kg/s. Reproduce the compressor power, the capacity in tons and β by hand from the state table.
